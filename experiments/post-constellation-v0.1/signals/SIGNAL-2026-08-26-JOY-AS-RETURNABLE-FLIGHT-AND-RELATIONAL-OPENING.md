## Candidate synthesis

### 1. Joy as trained retrieval

CAND: A person may be able to lower the access cost of a joyful or playful mode through repeated genuine cues and embodied returns, much as practice lowers the retrieval cost of a chord. This does not guarantee a mood or make joy appropriate in every scene.

```text
truthful state contact
→ genuine cue
→ embodied pulse
→ slightly wider response field
→ consequence and return
```

### 2. Flight-to-contact

CAND: A happy thought is useful when it creates enough lift to widen perspective and return to contact. It becomes avoidance when flight replaces grief, danger, responsibility, or necessary action.

```text
CONTACT → LIFT → WIDER VIEW → OFFER → RETURN → LAND
```

STORY: Peter Pan's happy-thought flight, Gear Five's elasticity, Joy Boy's laughter, and Super Saiyan held as base form carry different faces of this operation. They are not equivalent moral systems or psychological evidence.

### 3. Rhythm as a pre-semantic bridge

CAND: Broad tempo attunement may reduce uncertainty before language by establishing a minimally shared cadence. Exact mimicry is neither required nor safe; synchrony must remain emergent and interruptible.

### 4. Smile before greeting

CAND: A warm facial signal may be a smaller scene invitation than “hi.” It does not lower another person's guard as an operation performed on them. It gives them a low-cost path to reciprocate, ignore, or decline.

### 5. Relational distance is not physical distance

CAND: Two bodies can be spatially near while socially and attentionally far. Mutual recognition can briefly change some coordinates of relational distance without creating intimacy, trust, consent, or obligation.

### 6. Perspective adds a coordinate

CAND: Perspective-taking is representational flight with a landing requirement. It adds another position to the map; it does not erase the operator's pain, excuse conduct, or claim knowledge of another person's interior.

### 7. Lightness and weight are a dial

CAND: Lightness opens maneuver and contact. Weight sustains attention, responsibility, and consequence. Joy without weight risks Peter Pan drift; weight without play risks rigidity. Mature freedom can move between them.

## Practice candidate — Flight-to-Contact

```text
CONTACT → CUE → PULSE → SOFTEN → OFFER → READ → RELEASE → LAND
```

- CONTACT: name the actual state.
- CUE: retrieve one genuine source of lightness.
- PULSE: carry it through strum, step, hum, or breath.
- SOFTEN: reduce unnecessary force without dropping boundaries.
- OFFER: use one low-demand signal when appropriate.
- READ: distinguish reciprocity, ambiguity, and withdrawal.
- RELEASE: require no response and do not grade the person.
- LAND: return to body, environment, task, and observed consequence.

PRACTICE: Success is remaining truthful, warm, boundaried, responsive, and able to release—not making another person smile.

## Counterpaths and firewalls

1. Smiling can be cultural, situational, masked, involuntary, unsafe, or unwelcome; it is not a universal trust signal.
2. Eye contact can be uncomfortable or inappropriate across neurotypes, cultures, trauma histories, power differences, and contexts.
3. Exact gait or gesture imitation can feel invasive or mocking. Prefer field-level tempo awareness.
4. Joy does not solve structural, relational, medical, or material problems by itself.
5. Perspective-taking must not pressure injured people to excuse harm or surrender boundaries.
6. A non-response is not failure, rejection, or evidence about another person's character.
7. Do not make warmth into social engineering, charisma training, pickup technique, or permission to bypass consent.
8. Do not turn a callable playful mode into a demand for permanent happiness.
9. Story resonance is a carrier; lived practice and return supply calibration.

## Question portals

1. Can joy be practiced without becoming a performance?
2. How much of friendliness is a feeling—and how much is a rhythm you can practice until warmth arrives before self-protection does?
3. What is the smallest action that can turn two strangers passing into one shared moment?
4. When two people smile at each other, what distance actually changed?
5. Is a happy thought healthy because it lets you escape the moment—or because it helps you return to the moment with more options?
6. Can you see another person's side without making your own pain disappear?
7. What is the difference between becoming more joyful and becoming better at looking joyful?
8. If play makes you lighter and responsibility gives you weight, what does it mean to be able to move between both?
