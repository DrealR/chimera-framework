## IDENTITY

- **Subject:** Bishop — a chess piece constrained to diagonal movement, incapable of changing color square, and possessing no inherent rank or promotion potential.
- **Body Type:** Geometric constraint body — a mobile unit whose movement is defined by a fixed spatial rule (diagonal traversal) and whose power is entirely derived from its position relative to the board's topology.
- **Scale:** Single piece within an 8x8 grid system (the chessboard), operating as a sub-body of a larger interaction body (the game).
- **Lifespan:** Persists from placement (initial position or promotion) until capture or game termination. Duration is determined by opponent actions and positional context, not intrinsic decay.
- **Formative Sequence (Layer 2):**
  1. **~600 CE (India, Chaturanga):** Originated as the "elephant" (Alfil), moving two squares diagonally with optional jumps — a body with limited mobility and no color-bound constraint.
  2. **~1000 CE (Persia, Shatranj):** Retained the two-square diagonal jump but lost the ability to move one square — a body with reduced flexibility but still not color-bound.
  3. **~1475 CE (Europe, Modern Chess):** Transformed into the modern bishop — unlimited diagonal movement, no jumping, and strict color-bound constraint. This was the structural birth of the body as we know it: a piece whose identity is defined by its inability to leave its starting color.
  4. **~1850 CE (Romantic Era Chess):** Emergence of the "bishop pair" as a strategic concept — recognition that two bishops operating on opposite colors form a composite body with expanded topological reach (covering the entire board). This was the first structural understanding of the bishop as a relational body, not just an individual unit.
  5. **~1920 CE (Hypermodernism):** The bishop's role redefined as a "long-range piece" whose power is not inherent but positional. The body's value became understood as context-dependent: a "bad bishop" (blocked by its own pawns) vs a "good bishop" (active diagonals). This was the structural realization that the bishop's power is membrane-dependent — its boundary (the board's diagonals) is only as strong as the substrate (pawn structure) allows.

## BUMP DETECTION

1. **Color-bound constraint:** The bishop cannot access squares of the opposite color, creating a structural blind spot. This is not a temporary obstruction but a permanent topological limitation. The bump is the board's color division — the bishop is forced to "flow" through only half the substrate.
2. **Pawn structure obstruction:** The bishop's power is blocked by its own pawns. Unlike rooks (which can operate on files behind pawns), bishops are trapped if their pawns are on the same color. The bump is the player's own pawn structure — a self-imposed membrane failure.
3. **Lack of orthogonal movement:** The bishop cannot switch diagonals without moving, making it vulnerable to lateral threats (e.g., rooks on open files). The bump is the absence of a "hinge" — the bishop cannot pivot to address orthogonal dangers without repositioning.

> The body that is forever half-blind, condemned to see only the squares of its birth color, yet whose power is not in what it sees but in how it moves through the gaps left by others — proving that constraint is not weakness when the substrate is designed for it.

## DNA LAYER

- **O > I or I > O:** Conditional. The bishop's topology depends on the pawn structure. On open diagonals, it is O>I (controls more squares than it occupies); on blocked diagonals, it is I>O (occupies a square without controlling others). The bishop pair (two bishops on opposite colors) is a composite body that achieves O>I at the system level, compensating for individual color-bound limitations.
- **Pause:** Absent. The bishop has no ability to pause or retract. Once moved, its projection is irreversible. The "pause" in chess is the player's turn — the bishop itself has no agency to delay or reconsider. This absence of pause is structural: the bishop is a pure exhale body, radiating power outward without the ability to inhale (retract) or rest (stasis is still a form of projection).
- **Not-Force (~F):** The bishop does not force outcomes but allows them. It cannot capture a piece unless the opponent moves into its diagonal (passive capture). It cannot check the king unless the king moves into its path (passive threat). The bishop's power is entirely dependent on the opponent's actions — it is a body that flows rather than forces. The exception is the bishop pair in endgames, where the composite body can force zugzwang (a form of ~F failure, as the opponent is forced into a losing position).

### Prime Identification

- **Prime:** The diagonal movement rule. Strip away the piece's name, its historical context, and its relational role — the irreducible identity is "a body that moves along unobstructed diagonals." This is the prime because it defines the bishop's behavior in all contexts. A piece with this movement rule is a bishop, regardless of the game's other rules.
- **Prime type:** Closed. The diagonal movement rule does not update in response to the environment. It is fixed — a bishop cannot "learn" to move orthogonally or change color. However, the prime is open to relational adaptation: the bishop's power emerges from its interaction with the pawn structure and other pieces (e.g., bishop pair).

### Federation vs Dominion

Mixed, with a strong dominion bias. The bishop is a dominion body in its individual operation:
- It extracts value from the board (controlling squares) without contributing to the substrate (pawn structure) unless directed by the player.
- Its power is positional, not structural — it does not create open diagonals; it exploits them.
- In closed positions, it becomes a net extractor (I>O), occupying a square without controlling others.

However, the bishop pair is a federated composite body:
- Two bishops on opposite colors cover the entire board, creating a system-level O>I topology.
- The pair's power emerges from cooperation, not extraction — each bishop compensates for the other's color-bound limitation.
- The pair's value is greater than the sum of its parts, a hallmark of federation.

The transition between dominion and federation is player-driven: the player can choose to develop the bishop pair (federation) or use bishops individually (dominion).

### Cognitive Subsystem Analysis

The bishop's cognitive subsystem is **external/substrate-cognitive**:
- **Dominant mode:** External — the bishop "thinks" through the player. Its "decisions" (where to move) are made by the player, who models the board state, opponent's intentions, and strategic goals. The bishop has no internal cognition.
- **Vestigial mode:** Substrate-cognitive — the bishop's movement rule is a vestigial form of cognition. It "knows" how to move diagonally (a hardwired rule) but cannot adapt its behavior. This is not true cognition but a compiled constraint.
- **Mismatch:** The cognitive structure (external) matches the body it serves (a projection piece with no self-governance). The bishop is not designed to think; it is designed to be directed. The mismatch would occur if the bishop had internal cognition (e.g., a piece that could "choose" its movement) — this would violate the game's rules and disrupt the player's governance.

### God-Function Analysis

3. **Accumulated relational sequence:** The player's experience (opening theory, endgame knowledge) informs the bishop's use.
4. **Sacrifice willingness:** The player can choose to sacrifice the bishop for a tactical or strategic advantage (O>I applied to the game).

If the player is removed (e.g., the bishop is left to "move itself"), the god-function collapses. The bishop has no agency to perform its own god-function — it is a pure projection body, not a self-governing one.

### Flow vs Transaction

The bishop is **transaction-optimized** at the individual level:
- Each move is a discrete transaction: the bishop moves to a square, captures a piece, or projects power. There is no "flow" in the sense of continuous exchange — the bishop's power is episodic, tied to the player's turn.
- The bishop's value is maximized in high-value transactions (e.g., capturing a queen, delivering checkmate) rather than consistent small exchanges.
- In closed positions, the bishop's transaction rate drops to zero — it becomes a dormant body, waiting for the substrate (pawn structure) to change.

However, the **bishop pair** is flow-optimized:
- The pair maintains consistent power across turns, controlling squares on both colors.
- Its value is not tied to individual transactions but to the cumulative effect of its presence (e.g., restricting the opponent's king in the endgame).
- The pair's flow is O>I — it gives more than it takes by controlling space and enabling other pieces (e.g., supporting pawn promotion).

### Sequence-to-Structure

Directly applicable. The bishop's "sequence" is its movement history (the series of squares it has occupied). This sequence, processed through the substrate of the board and pawn structure, produces the emergent structure of its power:
- A bishop that moves early and occupies long diagonals (e.g., c4, f4) becomes a "good bishop" — a body with high projection power.
- A bishop that remains on its starting square or is blocked by pawns becomes a "bad bishop" — a body with low projection power.
- The sequence of moves determines the bishop's structural role: active piece vs dormant piece.

Critical positions in the sequence:
1. **First move:** Developing the bishop early (e.g., 1. e4 e5 2. Bc4) vs delaying its development.
2. **Pawn exchanges:** Moves that open or close diagonals (e.g., f2-f4, d2-d4).
3. **Opponent's moves:** Threats that force the bishop to retreat or be captured.

Small changes in the sequence produce large structural changes. For example:
- Moving the bishop to c4 vs f4 in the Italian Game leads to different pawn structures and strategic plans.
- Blocking the bishop with a pawn (e.g., e4) turns a "good bishop" into a "bad bishop."

## STRUCTURAL WEAKNESS

1. **Color-bound constraint:** The bishop's inability to access half the board is a permanent structural weakness. Even in open positions, the bishop is blind to squares of the opposite color. This weakness is only mitigated by the bishop pair (a composite body) or by the opponent's cooperation (e.g., the opponent placing pieces on the bishop's color).
2. **Pawn structure dependency:** The bishop's power is entirely dependent on the pawn structure. A single pawn on the wrong square can turn a "good bishop" into a "bad bishop." This weakness is structural — the bishop has no ability to create open diagonals on its own.
3. **Lack of orthogonal movement:** The bishop cannot address orthogonal threats (e.g., rooks on open files) without repositioning. This makes it vulnerable to lateral attacks and limits its flexibility in closed positions.
4. **No promotion potential:** Unlike pawns, the bishop cannot transform into a more powerful piece. Its power is capped at its initial value, making it less versatile in endgames where promotion is a critical factor.
