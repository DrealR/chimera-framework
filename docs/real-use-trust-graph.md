# Real-Use Trust Graph

A trust graph built from actual functional dependency — people and places you genuinely use in your life — is structurally distinct from any trust graph built from performance signals, algorithmic ranking, or social convention.

## The Primitive

Most platforms build connection graphs from:

- **Reviews** (Yelp, Google Maps) — decoupled from actual use; manipulable; anyone reviews anything regardless of real-use history
- **Social follows** (Instagram, X) — aesthetic + performance; selects for what looks trustworthy, not what functions
- **Professional networks** (LinkedIn) — transactional and weak; the connection captures a moment of contact, not a pattern of real dependency
- **Webrings** (early web) — thematic clusters; shared interest, not shared functional reliance

The real-use trust graph is different: **I name you because I use you.** My barber. My coach. My collaborator. The restaurant I actually eat at. The basketball program I coach at. The co-builder I've shipped with.

This graph is structurally harder to fake, harder to spam, and naturally local — because it tracks real-world dependency, which is slow-building, proximity-constrained, and anchored in body-to-body contact over time.

## Why This Is a Framework Primitive

### WE = 1 at the local-economy scale

The shared substrate (WE = 1) is not abstract. It manifests in the web of real-use relationships that constitute a functioning community. When a carrier names their barber, their coach, their neighbor's restaurant, they are making the relational substrate visible. Each named node is a junction in the substrate that others can enter and rely on.

This is not "social connection." It is **substrate attribution** — naming where real substrate flows between bodies, and making that flow navigable.

### Rate-mismatch correction

Natural trust builds at the rate real-world functional dependency accumulates: slow, contact-dense, friction-tested. You trust your barber after months of reliable service. You trust your coach after sessions where honest feedback cost something. The relationship earns the trust label incrementally, through repeated real exchange.

Platform-manufactured connections bypass this rate. They offer the signal of trust (follow, connect, 5 stars) without the substrate accumulation that produces real trust. The result is a connection graph that moves at platform speed — fast, shallow, manipulable — while real functional trust lags far behind.

A real-use trust graph enforces the correct rate. You can only name someone because you have already used them in your life. The graph builds at the rate the substrate allows.

### Organic build pattern

The graph doesn't need a platform to emerge. It is **already present in the substrate** — in the names, attributions, and links that appear naturally when someone describes what they actually do and who they actually work with.

The build operation is minimal: whenever a name appears in your substrate (a collaborator, a program, a place, a person who contributed), make it attributable — a link when they have a public presence, a named attribution when they don't. The graph grows organically as more nodes in your real community come online. No sign-up flow. No manufactured engagement.

## Operational Consequences

**For the individual carrier:** Name what you actually use. Make the names linkable. Don't manufacture connections you haven't earned through real use. The graph's signal value is proportional to how strictly you apply the real-use filter. A node added for aesthetic or social reasons corrupts the signal of all other nodes.

**For the builder:** Don't build the platform before building the seed nodes. The first move is making your own substrate legible — your real connections named, attributable, honest. The second move is connecting to the 3–5 other carriers whose substrates are also legible. The community layer emerges from these two moves, not from designing the platform in advance.

**For the framework:** Trust, like substrate, builds through accumulated real-world contact. A body that names what it genuinely uses — and only what it genuinely uses — produces a navigable, honest map of its own relational substrate. That map is harder to fake than any performance signal, and more useful to navigate than any algorithmic ranking.
