# The Turing Trap and the Scarce Question
*A framework study. The career thesis confirmed by the field's top digital-economy economist: the mistake is making AI imitate humans (the Turing trap, which removes the need for humans); the value is humans+machines doing NEW things together, and gains are biggest when the two are most DIFFERENT. Every problem is define-the-question + execute + evaluate — AI gets good at executing, so defining the right question and evaluating become the scarce, valuable human work. Graduated from a core capture. Internal register.*

## 0. The claim, stated once

**Don't make AI a perfect imitation of humans — that removes the need for them. Do new things WITH it. The gains-from-trade are biggest where human and machine are most different (build the spikes higher, don't sand them down to match). The scarce resource becomes defining the right question and evaluating the answer — so be the questioner and the evaluator, not the answer-taker, and stay in the loop, because being un-substitutable IS your leverage.**

---

## 1. The Turing trap vs the centaur

**Keeper:** The Turing trap is the urge to make AI a perfect imitation of a human. Imitation removes humans — if the machine does exactly what you do, there's no chair left for you. Complementarity multiplies humans — if the machine does what you can't and you do what it can't, you each get more valuable as the other improves. Gains-from-trade are biggest when the two entities are most DIFFERENT, so the move is to build the spikes higher, not to make AI human-like or to make yourself machine-like. The centaur — human + machine doing a new thing together — beats either one alone.

## 2. Define the question / execute / evaluate

**Keeper:** Every problem decomposes into three parts: define the question, execute the answer, evaluate the result. AI is rapidly taking execution. That pushes all the scarcity to the two ends — defining the right question and judging whether the answer is any good. "The scarce resource will be defining the right questions." This IS the AI-rewards-the-questioner / predict-then-evaluate thread, now validated as economic structure rather than just operator intuition: the economy will pay for the question and the judgment, not the keystrokes.

## 3. The call-center nuance — leveling is real, judgment is still earned

**Keeper:** AI leveled the call-center floor by helping novices most (30-40%) and experts ~0% — because the LLM distributed the experts' good answers to the novices. The leveling is real AND the judgment still ORIGINATES from mastery; someone earned it first, and the novice is borrowing it. "Never ship what you can't explain": borrowing the answer isn't owning the judgment — that's the borrowed-legs thread. The floor rises, but the source of what's being distributed is still a human who did the work. Be the source, not only the borrower.

## 3.5 Cheap execution, expensive questions

As cheap models get good enough at known tasks, execution becomes table stakes. The strategic split becomes:

- **Execution layer:** route known, bounded, evaluable work to cheap fast models.
- **Imagination / redirection layer:** spend frontier model calls, human attention, time, and risk on questions that change what the execution layer should do.

The Mitchell Hashimoto-style `$40 question` is the clean carrier image: the frontier model's answer mattered, but the scarce ingredient was the operator with enough domain context to ask the question at all. Most people would have used cheaper execution to move faster through the old backlog. The high-leverage operator asks whether the backlog is still the right body.

**Keeper:** when execution becomes cheap, the question becomes expensive.

**Firewall:** technical imagination is not fantasy. It is imagination constrained by fingertip familiarity with the domain, the tools, the costs, and the verification surface.

## 4. The dark end — why the seat matters

**Keeper:** Labor CAN be driven out of the production function — it is economically possible to run an economy where a few rich consume the output, the numbers add up, and there is no auto-correction. So keeping humans valuable is a CHOICE, not a law of nature, and concentrated wealth becomes concentrated power. Hold this as real structural risk, not doom (the tech is promising; the distribution is the danger). Your personal move is to be un-substitutable: the operator seat IS your bargaining leverage. Stay in the loop, do the thing the machine can't, and keep your hand on the question and the evaluation — that's the chair that doesn't get automated away.
