# Derivative Principle

A learner who has not yet named a skill is not empty of that skill. They are practicing it informally — in the domains where they spend attention, in the problems they already navigate, in the thinking they already do before any instruction begins. The formal skill is *derivative* of what already exists in their substrate. The teacher's first job is recognition, not transfer.

## The Observation

Lamont, working alongside Reemy on the Youth Builder Program, articulated this most directly: the kid in BeyondChess was *already doing computer science* before anyone called it that. The chess player tracking piece relationships across a dynamic board, anticipating two and three moves deep, modeling opponent intent — that is systems thinking, pattern recognition, state-space traversal. The formal vocabulary is new. The practice is not.

This holds at every scale of the teaching arc:
- A student in Makuyuni noticing that the dip-tank schedule fails her family's herd is already doing data-gathering, stakeholder identification, and problem-scoping — three of the six steps in the methodology.
- A young programmer who routes around a social problem with a WhatsApp group is already doing distributed-systems thinking without the name.
- A 7-year-old who organizes how neighborhood kids take turns with a basketball is already doing fairness-constraint algorithm design.

The informal practice precedes the instruction. The formal skill is derived from substrate that already exists.

## What Teaching Does Under This Principle

Teaching, correctly understood from this principle, performs three operations — and only three:

**1. Recognition.** The teacher observes until they can see where the learner's substrate already carries informal practice of the target skill. This requires attention-observation, not pressure-observation. The teacher is reading, not testing.

**2. Naming.** The teacher gives the informal practice its formal name. This is the moment of derivation — extracting the formal concept from the pre-existing substrate. "What you're doing when you organize the turns is called fairness-constraint design." The student already knew how to do it. They now know what they know.

**3. Extension.** Once the informal practice has a formal name, the teacher extends it: into new domains, into harder cases, into the language of the discipline, into tools that let it ship. This is where formal instruction begins — not at the start, but here, after recognition and naming.

The sequence is recognition → naming → extension, not instruction → practice → performance. This is the inversion of the blank-slate teaching assumption.

## The Blank-Slate Error

Most formal instruction operates from the assumption that the student arrives empty and the teacher fills them. This assumption produces the worst version of the teaching relationship: the expert deposits, the novice receives, and the student's pre-existing substrate is invisible — sometimes actively suppressed.

When a student's informal practice is suppressed rather than named, the cost is doubled: the student loses the anchor point they already had, and the teacher loses the fastest path to formal competence. Both parties work harder for a worse outcome.

The Derivative Principle is not a softening of instructional rigor. It is a structural correction. Recognizing what the student already knows is the most rigorous possible starting move — it requires genuine observation rather than curriculum delivery.

## The CHIMERA Origin

The framework itself was built this way. CHIMERA does not describe practices that practitioners must learn from scratch. It names what substrate-deep practitioners already do: scan bodies, locate primes, hold the Pause, calibrate friction, build O > I across relationships. The formal vocabulary names pre-existing practice. The framework's recognizability is evidence of this — the practitioner who encounters the framework and says "that is what I've always been doing" is the practitioner the framework was written for.

Teaching programs built from this principle are thus self-consistent with the framework: they teach by recognition, the same way the framework names what practitioners already carry.

## Pedagogical Consequence

The entry move for any instruction under this principle is observation before explanation. Not "what do you know?" — that asks for formal recall. Not "have you done this before?" — that asks for surface history. The right entry question is *"where have you already solved something like this?"*

The answer locates the learner's existing anchor. What follows is recognition + naming + extension. The learner's existing substrate is not a detour before instruction begins — it is the instruction substrate.

## Relation to Other Primitives

**Anchor-and-Transfer Principle:** Stage 1 of the anchor-and-transfer arc requires finding where the student already has anchor-substrate. The Derivative Principle explains WHY an anchor almost always already exists: because informal practice precedes formal instruction, and the informal practice has already built substrate.

**Teacher-Student Rhythmic Duality:** The teacher's first move — locating where the student is already free — is the recognition operation that the Derivative Principle names as primary. The rhythmic duality can only begin once the teacher has located the existing substrate; this principle explains how.

**Substrate Accumulation:** The informal practice IS accumulated substrate — the body has built depth through attention before the formal name arrives. Teaching does not begin accumulation; it makes existing accumulation legible and ship-worthy.

**Observation Pressure vs Attention:** Recognition requires attention-observation (gaze turned toward what the learner actually does). Pressure-observation (testing for correct performance before existing practice is visible) inverts the sequence and makes recognition impossible.

**Personal Primes as Invariants:** Biographical primes — irreducible life-scale anchors — exhibit the same physics: they are recognized and named, not invented. The events that become primes were already load-bearing in the substrate before anyone articulated their invariance. The Derivative Principle at biographical scale.

**Don't Chase Value, Produce Value:** The teacher who sees value in what the student already carries is applying O > I at community scale — contributing recognition, the most substrate-efficient teaching act. The teacher who arrives with the complete formal skill to deposit is chasing the wrong value location.
